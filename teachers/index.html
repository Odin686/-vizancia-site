Grades 6–8 · Free educator pilot · Classroom studio 2

Evidence to Action

Solve problems with AI. Keep your judgment.

Students start with their own thinking, inspect prepared AI replies, test claims against evidence, ask better questions and improve a recommendation they can defend.

Four planned 45-minute sessions plus an approximately eight-minute baseline. Paper is a complete route. No student accounts, live AI service or individual devices are required.

One problem, a visible thinking trail

AI contributes. Students explain and decide.

The fictional Campus needs to conserve water while keeping its garden healthy. Students compare options, investigate the records and produce a recommendation. Their report distinguishes what AI contributed from what they checked, changed or rejected themselves.

1. Think first

Define the problem, audience and success criteria. Preserve a first view before receiving help.

2. Brief the help

Choose a useful task for AI, a person or another tool. Specify sources, constraints and what remains the learner’s responsibility.

3. Report the contribution

Quote or accurately summarise what the prepared AI reply added, missed or overstated.

4. Question and test

Inspect original records, check a calculation and ask which assumptions or missing evidence could change the decision.

5. Iterate for a reason

Bring an independent finding into a better brief. Inspect the revision; more exchanges alone do not demonstrate learning.

6. Make the decision yours

Accept, edit or reject suggestions. Explain the final recommendation, its limits and who remains responsible.

Human skills students keep practising

Build understanding that remains when AI is put away.

Understanding and recall

Explain an idea in their own words. An optional later recall check asks students to retrieve an idea before reopening the records and correcting it.

Reasoning and evidence

Connect a claim to a precise source detail, check a calculation and explain what the evidence cannot establish.

Useful questioning

Identify assumptions, consider alternatives and ask a question that could change the recommendation.

Writing and explanation

Keep an initial view, make a consequential edit and explain the work orally or in writing. Polished wording alone is insufficient.

Ethical judgment

Consider permission, privacy, access and affected people. Keep responsibility for the final choice with people.

Reflection and transfer

Apply the routine to a new case before feedback, then distinguish an independent attempt from a supported revision.

Choosing not to use AI is a valid, explainable decision. The core uses fixed authored exchanges, including an improved reply that still needs human review. Agreed access supports remain available during independent work.

Learning design

Teach the strategy, model it and make room for independent practice.

Plan, monitor, evaluate

Students set criteria, inspect their progress and explain a revision. This makes their learning process visible for teacher feedback.

EEF guidance · 2025

Models and own attempts

Teacher examples and focused questions support the task. Students retain their own first attempts before worked answers and feedback.

IES practice guide · 2007

Explain, revise, revisit

Ask why a source supports a claim and what would change a decision. An optional delayed recall activity is additional to the four-session core.

IES learning recommendations

Purposeful AI use

Choose AI help for a learning purpose and inspect its contribution. Stronger task output does not by itself establish stronger understanding.

OECD outlook · 2026

These sources inform the design; they do not endorse or validate this packet. UNESCO’s student AI framework also informs the emphasis on human agency and responsibility. The pilot has not been evaluated for learning gains or validated as an assessment.

Your four-session sequence

  1. Frame the problem

    Read the garden challenge, preserve a first view, inspect the original records and write a bounded request for help.

  2. Audit the AI contribution

    Inspect prepared replies, report a useful contribution and a concern, then check the records independently.

  3. Question, iterate and decide

    Consider stakeholder needs, revise the brief using new findings, inspect a prepared revision and write the final recommendation.

  4. Apply the routine independently

    Use a new library case before substantive feedback. Preserve the first response, then explain a supported revision.

People, organisations, measurements and AI-style replies are fictional. The replies were not generated from students’ wording. The projection follows this sequence and takes no student input.

For teachers and school reviewers

What to review before a classroom trial.

Learning evidence

Use the first view, checking log, revised brief and final explanation. Feedback is descriptive. Keep independent work separate from coached revision.

Access and participation

Allow read-aloud, enlarged print, drawing, oral responses, scribing and extra paper. Do not assess speed, spelling or confidence as a substitute for reasoning.

Tools and privacy

The paper pathway requires no student login or live AI. An optional adult-operated demonstration needs local approval and fictional inputs. Student work stays with the school.

Privacy details

Local fit and timing

Review curriculum fit, accommodations, lesson time and any optional tool through your school’s process. Timings are planning estimates that need an educator dry run.

Publishing a free pilot does not establish board approval, a formal curriculum mapping or programme effectiveness. Share material feedback only; do not send student names, grades, private reflections or work samples.
